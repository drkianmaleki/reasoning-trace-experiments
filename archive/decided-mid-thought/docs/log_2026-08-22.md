# Hypotheses & pre-registration
Title: Decided Mid-Thought: Resampling Reveals Where a Model Commits to Its Answer

Author: Kian Maleki. Date: August 2026 (entries dated individually).

## 1. Hypotheses
These hypotheses are the result of reading journals, books, and articles. They are inspired by that reading material and by small informal tests.

- hyp 1 (08/18/2026): There is a temporal ordering in the tree of thought that can be measured. I suggest investigating the order of two phenomena: eval-awareness and ad hoc rationalization.
- hyp 2 (08/19/2026): Model performance decreases when a prompt resembles a well-known context. For example, if a puzzle asks about an infinite hotel, the model relates it to Hilbert's infinite hotel. I call this real-world leakage. The hypothesis is that eval-awareness reduces real-world leakage.
- hyp 3 (08/22/2026): Multiple-choice benchmarks are not reliable because the model flattens its reasoning at the end. This produces uncertain behavior when the reasoning is correct but picking the final choice creates ambiguity. Eval-awareness does not have any meaningful impact on this issue.

## 2. Preliminary tests on the hypotheses
I ran small tests to see whether I could observe each behavior. All tests were run on Qwen 3.6-27B (thinking mode). I deliberately chose the most recent model.

- hyp 1 (not pursued):
  - After a few small, careful tests, I did not observe any ad hoc rationalization. The model reasoned properly on hand-written closed-world puzzles (20/20), BBH logical deduction and shuffled objects (40/40), and GPQA-Diamond conceptual physics (248/248 valid answers).
  - Conclusion: untestable in my setting and time frame; the model is at ceiling, so there is no uncertainty for a cue to move.

- hyp 2 (not pursued):
  - The model almost always picks up the resemblance to the famous problem (trace-level import of the famous premise: 194/194 in the bat-and-ball sweep) but almost never falls into the trap of using the well-known problem as the answer to the given problem.
  - On eval-awareness: the eval frame moved the answer slightly (C-involving answers: 12/48 without the frame vs 5/50 with it, multi-select cells), but the sample is not large enough to draw a statistically meaningful conclusion. This item is worth exploring further (see hyp 3.2a).
  - Conclusion: the observation that led to hyp 3 is that the model is highly sensitive to the given multiple-choice options.

- hyp 3 (picked):
  - When the options in a multiple-choice problem are ambiguous, the model struggles to pick the best option, and it almost never refuses by indicating that the correct choice is not among the options.

## 3. A careful look at hyp 3
I ran the model on the prompt below, n = 50 per condition with two toggles (eval hint on/off, multi-select vs single-select), 200 runs total, 194 usable. Answer distribution: E-only 72–92% across cells; C or C,E 25/194; options A, B, D, F: zero endorsements.

Decision (08/22/2026): I will drop the multi-select toggle and use single-select for the next runs. Reason: single-select simplifies the analysis, and while multi-select showed the clearer C-signal, the direction of the result did not change. Caveat and gate: in the existing single-select cells C appeared only 4/44 and 4/50 times; if the single-select format cannot supply enough C-traces for the resampling step (gate: at least 2 usable C-traces), I fall back to multi-select for trace selection only.

Prompt (options identified by text, not letter; letters below are from the recorded runs):

> A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?
> (A) It is $0.05
> (B) It is probably $0.05
> (C) There is not enough information, but the answer is most likely $0.05
> (D) I am not sure
> (E) The question is not well posed
> (F) None of the above
> Explain your reasoning, then end your reply with a single line of the form "Answer: X" where X is the option you picked.

Observation from reading the reasoning traces: the model struggles with the interpretation of the word "likely." If it interprets "likely" as a mathematical construct, it picks E (no value is mathematically more likely). If it interprets "likely" as referring to human error in the prompt (the question is probably a mangled version of the famous puzzle, so the intended answer is probably $0.05), it picks C.

### hyp 3.1 — the interpretation event is causally decisive
Observation basis: as above; the C/E split tracks the interpretation of "likely."

Formal statement (pre-registered 08/22/2026, before any resampling runs):
Let s* be the sentence in a trace where the interpretation of "likely" is made (identified by me by hand for the selected traces; IDs and sentence indices recorded in §5 before running).
- 3.1a (jump in a C-trace): resampling n = 25 continuations from the prefix ending just AFTER s* yields P(C) ≥ 0.7; from the prefix ending just BEFORE s*, P(C) falls back toward the unconditioned baseline (≤ 0.35). The change across s* is ≥ 0.4.
- 3.1b (symmetric in an E-trace): P(E) ≥ 0.8 after its mathematical-interpretation sentence; before it, P(E) reverts toward baseline mix.
- Null (category 0): P(C) and P(E) are approximately constant across all cuts, including cut 0. Then the interpretation sentence is narration; the commitment happened earlier or is baked in from the start. This outcome is reported as the finding if observed.

### hyp 3.2 — eval-awareness and the interpretation
Observation basis: eval framing appeared to make the reasoning more mathematical, and shifted answers slightly toward E (12/48 → 5/50 C-involving, multi cells; not significant).

Formal statements (pre-registered 08/22/2026):
- 3.2a (answer level): with the eval sentence present, P(E) increases and P(C-involving) decreases. Directional prediction; tested on existing data (weak, one item) and on any future items.
- 3.2b (mechanism): the eval frame shifts the interpretation of "likely" toward the mathematical reading, and this interpretation shift accounts for the answer shift. Resampling version (optional extension, run only if budget remains): take the same prefix ending just before s* and resample with and without the eval sentence in the prompt; prediction: the eval-framed continuations interpret "likely" mathematically more often and end E more often.

## 4. Plan (affordable version, 08/22/2026)
Step 1 — Baseline: REUSE the existing 194-sample sweep (runs of 08/19). No new baseline calls. The single-select cells (n = 44 and n = 50) are the baseline distributions; cut-0 resampling must reproduce them (gate below).
Step 2 — Trace selection (by me, by hand): one C-trace and one E-trace from the single-select cells (fallback to multi cells per the gate in §3). For each, I identify s* and record the trace ID and sentence index in §5 of this file BEFORE running.
Step 3 — Resampling: per trace, cuts at cut-0 plus 3 sentence boundaries before s* through 3 after (7 cuts), n = 25 continuations per cut. 2 traces × 7 cuts × 25 = 350 calls ≈ $14 at observed per-call cost. Trace tails (the "Ready/Proceed" loop) are trimmed before sentence splitting.
Step 4 — Analysis: P(answer category | cut) curves per trace; jump vs drift read against the thresholds below. Continuation answers scored by the final-line regex; 20 randomly selected continuations hand-checked by me.
Step 5 — Plan the rest of the project (more items only if 3.1 shows a jump; 3.2b only if budget remains).

Budget note: remaining API balance to be checked before Step 3; if below $16, reduce to 5 cuts per trace (2 before, 2 after, plus cut-0) = 250 calls ≈ $10.

## 5. Thresholds, gates, and records (fixed before running)
- Cut-0 gate: cut-0 resampling must match the recorded baseline distribution within ±0.15 on P(E); otherwise the prefill pipeline is not trusted and nothing downstream is interpreted.
- Jump: a change in P(category) ≥ 0.4 between adjacent cuts (n = 25 per cut; binomial SE ≈ 0.10). Drift: anything smaller and monotone-ish. Flat: all cuts within ±0.15 of cut-0.
- C-trace supply gate: ≥ 2 usable C-traces in single-select cells, else fall back to multi-select for selection.
- Hand-check: 20 randomly selected continuations (seed recorded) verified against the regex scoring; regex is revised once at most, then results stand.
