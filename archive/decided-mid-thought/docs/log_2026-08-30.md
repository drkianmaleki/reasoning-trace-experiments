## Verdict: Null / category 0
This item tests to see if there is any meaningful change in the mid-thinking
cuts vs the cut at zero. Frozen test: "Flat: all cuts within ±0.15 of cut-0."
Rejection of this hyp means that the rest of the analysis and research can go
on, and if this hyp is confirmed it means that there is nothing to look for
here.

On C-trace, the hyp is rejected because there is a clear upward trend in C
counts vs cuts. C count at the baseline (cut-0) is 3/25 and it rises to 25/25
at cut-62 — far outside the ±0.15 band at many cuts. Post-hoc supporting
statistic: Fisher exact, cut-0 vs cut-62 (3/25 vs 25/25), p < 1e-8.

On E-trace, E count at the baseline (cut-0) is 20/25 (0.80), so the band tops
out at 0.95; the cuts sit at 0.96–1.00, so the E-arm formally exceeds the flat
band too. But substantively there was only 0.20 of headroom to begin with, so I
read the E-arm as a small drift toward ceiling, functioning as a control arm,
not as meaningful trace-driven commitment. (An earlier draft of this verdict
used 0.88 here — that is the sweep-cell baseline, 39/44, not cut-0; corrected.)

Overall, the cuts show a decisive and meaningful trend that is worth exploring,
and the C-trace alone is enough evidence to refute this hyp. For better
hypotheses I have decided to base future hyps on pre-registered region
contrasts tested with Fisher's exact test — more on that in the lessons.

This hyp is refuted but it teaches us a few interesting observations.
1. The model seems to mostly stick to C after cut-60. Is there anything
   specific about sentence 60 that contains the word "likely"?
2. There is a rise in P(C) from 0.12 to 0.60 (from cut-29 to cut-59) that
   happens BEFORE the interpretation of the word "likely" appears in the text
   (first hedge-vocabulary engagement is s58). This raises the question: what
   happens between sentences 29 and 59? The Amendment-2 result below is the
   answer at region resolution.
3. Further study of the C-trace and this hyp shows that there is no localized
   change (jump) in the cuts, and the commitment to the final response happens
   gradually. This opens up the following questions. Are models with gradual
   commitment better models than models that show a localized jump in their
   decision making? Is it worth designing a benchmark to categorize models by
   the existence of jumps in their reasoning? Another point of view is that we
   are analyzing the NARRATIVE of the reasoning, and the commitment and the
   interpretations are not necessarily reflected in the narrated reasoning.

What is refuted here is the LOCALIZATION of commitment at the interpretation
sentence. Whether the reading of "likely" a continuation adopts predicts its
answer (the mediation question) was not tested and stays in future work.

## Overall classification (section-5 vocabulary)
C-trace: category 0 rejected; no jump at sentence resolution; DRIFT,
NON-MONOTONIC, with one pre-specified region (s30–s44) clearing the change
threshold and saturation (≥ 0.88, mostly 1.00) from s60 onward. E-trace:
near-ceiling throughout; prompt-determined default. The headline findings:
(1) distributed, non-monotonic in-trace construction of the minority answer;
(2) the default/minority asymmetry; (3) post-saturation elaboration — 84.6% of
the C-trace's characters are generated AFTER cut-60 (the registered s*), by
which point P(C) is at 0.88 and never falls below it; anchored at the first
25/25 cut (s62) the figure is 83.9%. In plain words: once the answer
distribution has effectively saturated, the LM keeps thinking for another five
sixths of the trace without changing where it lands.

## Lessons for the next pre-registration
1. Parameter naming and cut semantics must be defined more rigorously up front
   (cut-k, s*, region vs sentence — the ambiguity cost me a wrong first
   verdict before I caught it).
2. The hypothesis should be shape-formed: pre-registered region contrasts,
   each tested with Fisher's exact test, instead of a single-sentence jump.
   (A trend test like Cochran–Armitage was considered and dropped: it tests
   monotone trend and this curve is non-monotonic; chosen now it would be
   post-hoc anyway.)
3. Verdict clauses are sensitive to cut spacing — alternative spacings around
   the same data flip which clauses pass — so spacing is part of the
   hypothesis and should be justified in advance.
4. The one-revision clause for the classifier earned its place: the seeded
   hand-check found real defects and the revision path was clean because it
   was pre-specified.

## Exploratory observations (post-hoc, reading-only; no claims)
- Two temporal-ordering hypotheses (C = math-first-then-recognition; and its
  inverse) were proposed from my reading and REFUTED by a vocabulary scan of
  six same-cell traces: all six are recognition-first (C-traces recognize at
  s0, s0, s16; E-traces uniformly at s14 inside a shared scaffold). My initial
  reading had anchored on later discursive resemblance passages and missed
  terse early recognitions — recorded as a bias instance caught by the check I
  requested.
- Surviving candidate patterns from that reading (indication only, n = 3+3):
  (i) opening-mode homogeneity — the E-traces share a near-identical opening
  template while the C-traces open in three different modes;
  (ii) constraint-as-given adoption — one C-trace (_020) initially imports the
  missing "$1.00 more" into its algebra as a given.
- A candidate motivating citation for step-ordering (arXiv 2606.11926) was
  checked and found unrelated (autonomous research agents); not cited.
- A new idea, inspired after realizing that a temporal ordering and a localized
  jump do not exist: a TOY MODEL, in the physicist's sense of the word — an
  effective description built to reproduce the measured behavior, with no claim
  about what the language model actually does internally, and no claim that it
  "thinks." (Careful with the word "model" here: below, "toy model" means my
  simulation; "the LM" means the language model under study.) The toy model:
  each analysis STATEMENT (not sentence) goes into a box, and each statement
  carries a weight. The output of the box is the answer: an answer is drawn at
  random according to what is in the box; then the next statement is added
  (along with the previous result), each with its own weight, and a new draw is
  made. At some point the box is so packed that adding a statement no longer
  alters the draw — yet statements keep being added and the same answer keeps
  being drawn. Fitting the observed curves would require the early statements
  to carry higher weights than statements added at later stages (a primacy
  weighting). The toy model is judged the way effective models are judged in
  physics: by whether it reproduces the measured answer-distribution dynamics
  and makes a testable prediction — here, that answer distributions should be
  far more sensitive to rearranging EARLY statements than late ones. That
  prediction can be tested by SHUFFLED RESAMPLING: permute the first few
  statements of a prefix and resample. One design caveat for later — shuffled
  prefixes are off-distribution text the LM never produced, so results would
  need the same care as any off-policy intervention.

## Future work
1. Mediation labeling on the cut-58/59 continuations: within identical
   prefixes, does the adopted reading of "likely" predict the final answer?
   (The live form of the mechanism hypothesis.)
2. 3.2b: eval-frame on/off resampling from the same pre-s* prefix.
3. Explore the shuffled-resampling idea (box model above).
4. Labeling dimensions from the exploratory reading: opening mode,
   constraint-as-given, verbalized-recognition timing.
5. Minimal-pair rewrite of option C, to separate register ambiguity from
   underspecification. In the current item these are confounded: the puzzle is
   missing a premise AND option C is worded with a hedge ("most likely") that
   has both a mathematical and an everyday reading, so long deliberation and
   the C/E split cannot be attributed to either cause. Fix: two versions of the
   item identical except for option C's wording — one forcing the everyday
   reading ("... but the intended answer is $0.05"), one forcing the
   mathematical reading ("... but no value has higher probability") — same
   math, same missing premise. If answer distributions and trace lengths move
   with the wording, register ambiguity does causal work; if nothing moves,
   underspecification alone explains the behavior.
6. A second underspecified famous item (a different famous puzzle with one
   premise deleted), to test whether the findings are properties of this class
   of problems or quirks of the bat-and-ball specifically. Was in the original
   plan; cut for time.
7. A second model (Nemotron was cut early), to test whether this is Qwen 3.6
   behavior or reasoning-model behavior.
8. Activation probes for the pre-token disposition. The E-arm shows the answer
   is ~80% set before any thinking token exists, so no truncation method can
   locate it — there is no text to cut. A linear probe trained on the LM's
   hidden state at the prompt's final token, predicting the eventual answer,
   would test whether the disposition is linearly decodable before thinking
   begins. That is a mechanistic-interpretability step, deliberately beyond
   this behavioral study's boundary.
